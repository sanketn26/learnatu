# Doctors and nurses

A discharge note has to go home with a family who does not read clinical English. You want plainer words. A chatbot — ChatGPT, Gemini, or a similar program that rewrites in ordinary language — will offer them in seconds. That is a fair use *if* the note has no patient in it, and *if* a qualified person still checks medication, dose, and warning lines.

AI can help draft general communication and explain complex language. It must not replace clinical judgment, approved systems, or patient consent. It is not a diagnosis. It has not examined anyone.

---

## A safe learning path

1. **Use fictional examples.** Remove names, dates, identifiers, contact details, images, and unusual facts that could identify a patient. “A 60-year-old with high blood pressure” is a category. A ward, a rare diagnosis, and a date of admission together can be a person.
2. **Draft general instructions.** Use AI for a general-language template, then check it against your organisation's approved guidance. The template is a starting point. The protocol on the ward is the authority.
3. **Translate cautiously.** Have a qualified person verify medication, dosage, consent, discharge, and emergency wording. A missing “do not” in another language is not a small error.
4. **Verify medical claims.** Check authoritative clinical sources and local protocols. Do not rely on citations generated by AI. Chatbots invent paper titles and attach real authors’ names to them.

---

## Try this prompt

A **prompt** is the message you type into the tool:

> Rewrite this general, non-patient-specific instruction in plain language for an adult reader. Preserve every warning. If anything is ambiguous, list it instead of guessing: [approved general text]

---

## What this must not decide

Never use an AI answer alone to diagnose, prescribe, triage, calculate a dose, or make an emergency decision. Fluency is not an examination. If the situation would change treatment, look at the patient, the chart, and the protocol — not at the chat box.

A public chatbot is also not your organisation’s record. Pasting a discharge summary into a personal account can break confidentiality even when your intention was only to simplify the language.

---

## Never share with a public AI tool

- Patient names, images, reports, prescriptions, IDs, or contact details
- Appointment records or combinations of facts that identify someone
- Login details or content from a restricted clinical system
- Information your organisation has not approved for external processing

If your hospital or clinic provides an approved tool inside its own systems, use that rather than a personal ChatGPT or Gemini login. “Improve the model for everyone” — a ChatGPT setting that lets the company use chats to train, or teach, its software — has no place for clinical text. Turn it off on any personal account you still use for *non-patient* drafting, and keep patient work out of that account entirely.
