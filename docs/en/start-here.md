# Start here

You do not need to become “good at technology.” You need six habits that close the doors scammers walk through most often.

Read this once. Do the first three today if you can. The rest can wait until this week.

---

**Step 1 — Lock your phone**

Your phone is not just a phone. It holds your photos, your bank apps, your WhatsApp chats, and the messages banks send you to prove it is really you.

Set a PIN, a pattern, or a fingerprint lock. Without one, anyone who picks the phone up — a stranger in a shared auto, a visitor at home, someone who finds it on a seat — can read everything and act as you.

Open your phone’s settings, look for **Lock screen** or **Security**, and turn a lock on. If a family member already knows a weak PIN like `1234` or your birthday, change it.

**Step 2 — Install apps only from the official store**

An “app” is a small program on your phone: WhatsApp, GPay, a torch, a game. The safe place to get one is the **Play Store** (Android) or the **App Store** (iPhone). Those stores check, imperfectly, that the app is what it claims to be.

A link in WhatsApp or SMS that says “Install this to receive your refund” is how fake apps arrive. You open it, it looks real, and it can watch your screen or empty an account.

If you need an app, search for it yourself inside Play Store or App Store. Check the developer name. The official BHIM app is by NPCI. A copycat will steal the logo and use a different name.

**Step 3 — Never share an OTP**

An **OTP** is a one-time password: a short code, usually 4 to 8 digits, that a bank, UPI app, or phone company texts you. It is meant to prove *you have this phone in your hand, for this one action, right now*.

Your bank will never call you and ask you to read that code aloud. Neither will the government, the police, or “customer care.” Anyone who asks for it on a call is trying to complete a payment or a login as you.

When an OTP arrives, do not read it out. Do not type it into a page you reached from a message. Use it only inside the app you opened yourself.

**Step 4 — Protect your email password as if it opens every other door**

Your email (Gmail, Yahoo, a company address) is how most other accounts prove it is you. If someone gets into email, they can tap **Forgot password** on your bank, shopping, and social accounts and reset them.

Use a password for email that you do not use anywhere else. A short sentence is stronger than a name and a year: `MyTeaStallOpensAt7!` is far harder to guess than `ramesh1968`.

[How to make a safer password →](basics/passwords.md)

**Step 5 — Pause before you tap a link or paste private text**

Two everyday moments cause a lot of harm.

One: a message arrives with a link. “Your KYC is pending.” “Your parcel could not be delivered.” “See this photo of you.” Your thumb is already moving. Pause. Type the company’s name into Google or open the app yourself instead of tapping.

Two: you copy something from a work file, a customer list, a medical report, or a family document and paste it into a chat box or an AI tool to “make it clearer.” Ask: would I be all right if a stranger read this? If no — stop. Remove names and numbers first, or do not paste it.
