# Understand your money

The salary SMS arrives. For an hour the number looks large. Then rent, groceries, the school van, medicine, and the monthly instalment on the scooter — people call that an **EMI** — and by the 20th you are opening the bank app twice a day, wondering where it went.

That feeling is not a character flaw. Money arrives in one lump and leaves in a dozen directions, some of them without a name until they are gone. Financial planning starts here: a one-month picture of what comes in, what must go out, and what changes. You do not need a complicated spreadsheet. A page in a notebook, or a note on the phone, is enough.

## Make a one-month picture

Write four lists. Use last month if you remember it, or this month as it unfolds.

1. **Income.** Salary, wages, shop or business takings, a pension, government benefits, and any other money you can usually count on. If a relative sometimes sends help, keep it off this list for now. Why: a plan built on a lucky month will break on an ordinary one.

2. **Essential expenses.** Food, housing, electricity and water, daily transport, medicine, school costs, and the minimum payments on any loan or card. These are the costs that, if skipped, create trouble quickly. Why: until these have names and rough amounts, every other money decision is a guess.

3. **Flexible expenses.** Purchases that can change or wait — extra meals out, new clothes, a gadget, gifts that are not urgent. Why: this is the list you can actually adjust when a month is tight, but only if you can see it separately from rent and rice.

4. **Irregular expenses.** Annual fees, scooter or fridge repairs, festivals, insurance, school admission, a trip home. Take each yearly cost and divide it by twelve, so it has a monthly amount. Why: these are the bills that feel like emergencies only because they were never given a place in the month.

If your income varies — daily wages, delivery work, a shop, freelance jobs — plan using a cautious lower-income month, not the best month you had this year. Extra in a good month can be a later decision. A plan that assumes the good month every time will run out.

AI tools can help you sort headings if you type things like “rent, groceries, school fees” with no names attached. Do not upload bank statements, account numbers, payslips, tax documents, or identity records to a public AI tool. Those papers are keys. The tool does not need them to give you a blank list of categories.

> **Outcome:** I can say, in ordinary words, where my money comes from and where it has to go.

## Do this tonight

Open the notes app, or a page at the back of a school notebook. Write the four headings. Fill in what you know. Leave blanks. Tomorrow’s bank SMS will help you fill one of them.
