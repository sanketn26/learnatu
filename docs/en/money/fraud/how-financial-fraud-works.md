# How financial fraud creates pressure

The phone rings while you are at work. The voice is calm. They already know your name. They say your **KYC** is pending — that is the bank’s “know your customer” identity check, a process banks already do without calling you at lunch. If you do not “verify” in the next ten minutes, the account will be frozen. An **OTP** is about to arrive, they say: a one-time password, a short code that means the person holding this phone agrees to this one action. They would like you to read it out.

Nothing about that call is a conversation. It is a sequence.

Financial fraud is usually a sequence, not one clever message. The criminal first gains attention, creates trust or fear, isolates the person, and then demands an action — a tap, a scan, a code, a transfer.

## Common pressure tools

You will not see all of these every time. You will see a combination.

- **Urgency:** act now or lose the opportunity, the refund, the job, the account. Real banks and police do not run your life on a ten-minute clock. The clock is there so you do not think.
- **Authority:** bank, police, employer, regulator, or government. A logo, a uniform on a video, a number that *looks* like the bank’s. Authority is a costume. It is cheap to copy.
- **Emotion:** fear, greed, affection, embarrassment, or helpfulness. You are protecting a parent. You are about to miss a once-only return. You do not want the neighbour to know you were fooled.
- **Secrecy:** do not tell family, bank staff, or colleagues. “This is a confidential investigation.” Isolation is the point. The moment a second person hears the story, the costume slips.
- **Small compliance:** click, scan, install, share screen, or make a test payment. The first yes is designed to be small so the second yes feels like a continuation, not a new decision.
- **Sunk cost:** pay again to recover earlier money. The first loss is used as a hook. The second payment is not a way back. It is the next chapter of the same fraud.

Priya handles billing in a small office. A caller said her employer’s GST was flagged and a “token payment” would close the file. She almost paid because the voice used the company’s real name, which is public. She put the phone face down, walked to her manager, and called the accountant on a number they already had. There was no flagged GST. The sequence broke when a second person was allowed into the room.

!!! danger "Do not"
    - Act immediately just because a message or caller creates urgency or fear.
    - Keep the request secret from family, bank staff, or colleagues.
    - Trust a caller ID, logo, document, or voice as proof of identity.

The safest interruption is to end the conversation and verify through a channel you already trust: the number on the back of the card, the bank’s own app opened by you, a colleague you can see, a family member in the next room.

> A familiar voice, caller ID, logo, document, or video is evidence to check—not proof.

## If this call is happening now

Hang up. Do not explain. Open the official app yourself, or turn the card over and dial the printed number. Tell one person in the house what the caller wanted. If money already left, or you already shared an OTP, call the bank, then **1930**, and report at [cybercrime.gov.in](https://www.cybercrime.gov.in).
