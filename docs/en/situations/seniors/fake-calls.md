# Fake calls and messages

You are at home. The phone rings. The caller says they are from SBI, or from the police, or from “Aadhaar department.” They already know your name. They want a code, a PIN, or a small payment “to keep the account safe.” Put the phone down on the table. Nothing genuine is decided in the next thirty seconds.

**Who this is for:** Seniors and their families who want to recognise calls and messages from fraudsters pretending to be from banks, police, or government departments.

**Risk level:**

!!! danger "Never"
    Never share an OTP, PIN, account number, or Aadhaar details with anyone who called you. A real bank or government officer will never ask for these over the phone.

---

## What could go wrong

- A caller says "I am from the bank, your account will be blocked — share your OTP to save it." An **OTP** is a one-time password: a short code the bank texts you. Reading it aloud can let the caller complete a payment from your account.
- A message says "You have won a lottery — pay ₹500 to claim your ₹50,000." There is no lottery.
- A "policeman" calls and says you are in legal trouble. He asks for money via **UPI** — the system behind apps such as GPay, PhonePe, and BHIM — to "close the case quietly."
- A video call shows a person in police uniform — it could be a costume bought online.
- A message with a government logo says your Aadhaar is linked to a crime. It is fake.

---

## A safe prompt

Do not use AI here. When in doubt, end the call.

**Human steps instead:**

1. End the call without agreeing to anything.
2. Call back the bank using the number on the back of your card, or call a trusted family member.
3. Do not call back the number that called you — it may be a scammer's number.

---

## How to verify a call is real

1. Hang up. Call the official number (bank card, gov.in website). Ask if they tried to reach you.
2. Ask a family member to check whether the claim is real before you do anything.
3. A real government notice comes by post or registered email — not a WhatsApp message.

---

## Checklist

If a call or message has just arrived, walk through this once:

- [ ] Did you end the call without sharing any number or code?
- [ ] Did you call the official number to verify?
- [ ] Did you tell a family member about the call?
- [ ] Did you not press any number or "confirm" anything on a call you did not initiate?
- [ ] If money was already sent, did you call your bank immediately?

---

## Privacy settings to use

- **WhatsApp:** Settings → Privacy → Last seen → Nobody. That hides the clock that shows when you were last on the app, so a stranger cannot wait for the moment you pick up the phone.
- **Phone calls:** Enable "caller ID and spam" in your phone's settings to flag known scam numbers. It is not perfect; a new number will still ring. Treat an unexpected official-sounding call as unverified until you call back on a number you found yourself.
- **SMS:** Report spam SMS to TRAI by forwarding to 1909.
- **Do Not Disturb:** Register at [dndregister.trai.gov.in](https://dndregister.trai.gov.in) to reduce unsolicited calls.
- **Bank app:** Enable transaction alerts for every debit so you know immediately if money leaves.

## A realistic failure

Sixty-eight-year-old Ramesh received a call from someone claiming to be from “SBI Fraud Prevention.” The man knew Ramesh’s full name and the last four digits of his card — details that leak in small ways, from old forms and from companies that do not guard lists well. He said the account was flagged and Ramesh needed to “verify” himself by sharing the OTP that would arrive on his phone.

The code arrived. Ramesh, frightened, read it out. ₹42,000 left the account in seconds. The bank had not called. The person who knew a little about him had used it to sound like the bank.

If Ramesh had ended the call and dialled the SBI helpline printed for customers (1800 11 2211, free), he would have confirmed in two minutes that no one from SBI had called him. The code was never meant to be spoken. It was meant to stay on the phone.
