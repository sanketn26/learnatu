# Children's privacy

Sports Day, a birthday cake, the first day of Class 1. You take a photo because you love them. You post it because the grandparents are in another city, and the class WhatsApp group is already full of the same moment. Most of this is affection. Some of it is a map: a uniform, a school name on a gate, a building in the background that shows the neighbourhood.

**Who this is for:** Parents who post about their children on social media or share photos in WhatsApp groups.

**Risk level:**

!!! warning "Caution"
    Most parents share with good intentions, but photos of children can be copied, altered, or used to build a profile of where a child lives and goes to school.

---

## What could go wrong

- A photo tagged with a school name and uniform tells a stranger exactly where your child is every weekday morning.
- A birthday post with your child's full name, age, and city is enough for identity fraud when the child is older — someone using those details to open accounts or pretend to be them.
- A photo shared in a parent WhatsApp group reaches people you do not know because someone forwarded it.
- A child grows up and finds that years of their life are publicly searchable without their consent.

---

## A safe prompt

There is no AI tool involved here. The safer action is a pause before posting.

**Ask yourself before every post:**

- Does this show my child's school name or uniform?
- Does this reveal my neighbourhood or home?
- Would my child be comfortable with strangers seeing this at age 18?

---

## How to verify your current exposure

1. Open your Facebook or Instagram profile while logged out (or in a private browser window). What can a stranger see? **Public** means anyone on the internet. **Friends only** (sometimes called a private account) means only people you have accepted.
2. Search your child's full name on Google. What appears?
3. Check your WhatsApp groups — who are all the members, and do you know each of them personally?

---

## Checklist

Before the next photo leaves your phone, walk through this once:

- [ ] Did you remove the school name or uniform from the visible photo?
- [ ] Is your social media account set to "Friends only" or "Private"?
- [ ] Did you avoid using your child's full name in a public caption?
- [ ] Have you told grandparents and family not to post photos without asking you first?
- [ ] Do the parents in your school WhatsApp group all know each other personally?

---

## Privacy settings to use

- **Instagram:** Settings → Privacy → Private account. New people then need your approval before they can see posts, rather than finding them by searching.
- **Facebook:** Settings → Privacy → Who can see your future posts → Friends. New posts go to people you have accepted, not to the whole internet. Check past posts too; an old setting can still be Public.
- **WhatsApp:** Settings → Privacy → Profile photo → My contacts. Only people in your address book see the face on your profile.
- **Google Photos:** Do not share albums publicly; use the "share with specific people" option so a link cannot be opened by whoever receives a forward.
- **School communications:** Ask the school not to publish photos publicly without parental consent forms.
- **Location tags:** Turn off location tagging before posting photos on any platform. A location tag is a pin on a map; it can show the school gate or the house more precisely than the picture itself.

---

## A realistic failure

Rekha regularly posted photos of her daughter Siya on Facebook — Sports Day, birthdays, the walk to school — and tagged the school and the locality each time. She believed only friends could see them. Years earlier she had changed a setting and forgotten it. The account was **Public**: anyone who searched could open the album.

A relative discovered this when they typed Siya’s name into Google and found five years of photos, her school address, and the family’s home neighbourhood, all visible without logging in. Rekha switched the setting to “Friends only.” It took two minutes.

Those two minutes were available before the first photo. Siya had never been asked whether she wanted her childhood searchable. The setting, not the affection, was the problem.
