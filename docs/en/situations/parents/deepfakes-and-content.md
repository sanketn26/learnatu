# Deepfakes and harmful content

Another parent calls. There is a video in the school WhatsApp group that looks like your child — or a classmate. The face is familiar. The words, or the scene, are not. Your first instinct may be to tap the video, upload it somewhere to “check if it is fake,” and forward it to family so they are warned. Slow down. Forwarding is how it travels. Uploading it to a checker is how a private image becomes someone else’s copy.

A **deepfake** is a photo or video that has been digitally altered so it looks as if a real person said or did something they never did. The face can be taken from a school photo, a social media profile, or a Sports Day clip.

**Who this is for:** Parents who have found, or suspect, fabricated images or videos involving their child or someone they know.

**Risk level:**

!!! danger "Never upload"
    Do not upload images of the affected person to any AI tool or website to "check" whether they are fake. Report the content directly using the steps below.

---

## What could go wrong

- A photo taken from a child's social media profile is used to create a fabricated image.
- A video call recording is manipulated to show someone saying things they never said.
- A fabricated image is shared in a school WhatsApp group and spreads before anyone can stop it.
- The child or family member feels unable to tell a parent because they are ashamed, even though they did nothing wrong.

---

## A safe prompt

Do not use AI here. Chatbots and “fake-checkers” cannot reliably detect deepfakes, and uploading the image creates a new privacy risk: the file now sits on someone else’s computer.

**Human steps instead:**

1. Screenshot the post URL (not the image itself) as evidence. The **URL** is the address of the post — the line that starts with `https://` — so you can show *where* it appeared without spreading the picture.
2. Report the content to the platform (WhatsApp, Instagram, YouTube) using the Report button.
3. Call the school if it involves classmates.
4. Contact the police cybercrime helpline: **1930** or [cybercrime.gov.in](https://www.cybercrime.gov.in).

---

## How to verify the content is fabricated

1. Look at the face carefully — unnatural blinking, mismatched lighting, or blurry edges around the face are common signs. None of these is proof on its own, and a careful fake may show none of them.
2. Call the person in the video directly, on a number you already have, and ask if they recorded it.
3. Check whether the video or image appeared first on a platform known for fake content.

---

## Checklist

Before you do anything else with the file, walk through this once:

- [ ] Did you take a screenshot of the URL (not the image) as evidence?
- [ ] Did you report the post to the platform directly?
- [ ] Did you tell the child it is not their fault and they did the right thing by telling you?
- [ ] Did you contact the school if classmates are involved?
- [ ] Have you filed a report at cybercrime.gov.in?

---

## Privacy settings to use

- **Instagram:** Settings → Privacy → Private account. That switches the account so only people you approve can see photos, instead of anyone on the internet.
- **Facebook:** Profile → Edit privacy → Past posts → Friends only. That pulls old posts back from **Public** (visible to strangers) to people you have accepted as friends.
- **WhatsApp:** Settings → Privacy → Profile photo → My contacts. Strangers who have your number then see a blank or limited photo, not a clear face they can copy.
- **Google:** Search your child's name periodically and request removal of unwanted results via Google's removal tool.
- **School group chats:** Ask the admin to restrict who can send messages, so a fake account cannot drop a file into a group of forty parents.

---

## A realistic failure

Fourteen-year-old Arun’s class photo sat on his school’s public Facebook page. Anyone could save it. A fake account used it to create a fabricated video and posted it in his school’s WhatsApp group. Within an hour, forty students had seen it.

His mother found out when another parent called. She panicked and forwarded the video to her family to “warn them.” Each forward made a new copy. The people who wanted it gone now had to chase it through extra chats.

The pause that would have helped: screenshot the URL, report it to WhatsApp, call the school, and not forward the content at all. Arun had done nothing wrong. The photo on a public page had done the work for the person who made the fake.
