# Homework help

It is 10 p.m. The project is due in the morning. Your child is at the table with the textbook open and a chatbot on the phone — ChatGPT, Gemini, or another tool that writes answers in ordinary language. A **chatbot** is a program you type a question into; it replies as if a person were on the other side. There is not. It can explain a chapter. It can also invent a date and say it with complete calm.

**Who this is for:** Parents whose children ask to use Google, ChatGPT, or Gemini for school assignments.

**Risk level:**

!!! warning "Caution"
    AI tools can help, but they invent facts. Every answer needs a check before it goes into a submission.

---

## What could go wrong

- The chatbot gives a plausible-sounding but completely wrong historical fact, and the child submits it as true.
- The child copies a paragraph word for word. At many schools that counts as **plagiarism** — submitting someone else’s words as your own, including words a program wrote.
- The child photographs a test paper or homework sheet and uploads it. That question bank is now on a company’s computers.
- A chatbot in conversation mode asks personal questions, and the child answers freely — name, school, city, what the family does.
- The child learns to accept the first answer without questioning it. That habit follows them into exams, where the chatbot is not sitting next to them.

---

## A safe prompt

A **prompt** is the message you type into the tool. Ask it to explain, not to hand over a finished answer:

> "Explain how photosynthesis works in simple words. Do not make up any details. If you are not certain about something, say so."

Then have the child check the explanation against their textbook or a trusted site such as [ncert.nic.in](https://ncert.nic.in). **NCERT** is the organisation that writes many of the textbooks used in Indian schools; that site is their official one.

---

## How to verify the answer

1. Open the child's textbook and find the same topic. Do the key facts match?
2. Search the topic on a government or school board website (ncert.nic.in, byjus.com, or the school's own resources).
3. Ask the class teacher if the answer looks different from what was taught.

---

## Checklist

Before the work goes in, walk through this once:

- [ ] Did the child read and understand the AI answer, not copy it unseen?
- [ ] Did they check one fact against the textbook?
- [ ] Did they avoid uploading the question paper or test sheet?
- [ ] Does the submitted work sound like the child wrote it?
- [ ] Did the child mark which parts used AI (if their school requires it)?

---

## Privacy settings to use

- **ChatGPT:** Settings → Data controls → turn off "Improve the model for everyone." That setting, when it is on, lets the company use what you type to train — that is, to teach — its software. Turning it off keeps the chat off that path.
- **Google Gemini:** Activity controls → turn off Gemini Apps Activity. That stops Google from saving the conversation in the child's activity history.
- **Browser:** Use a browser that doesn't auto-fill passwords into AI chatbots, so a saved bank or email password is not dropped into the wrong box.
- **School account:** Use a school-provided account if available — it often has extra protections a personal login does not.
- **Age check:** Most AI tools require users to be 13+. Supervise children under 13 rather than creating an account in their name.

## A realistic failure

Priya’s daughter Meera, 11, had a history project due the next morning. She asked ChatGPT “When did the Mughal Empire end?” and got a confident answer with a specific year. She wrote it into the project and went to bed feeling finished.

Her teacher marked it wrong. The chatbot had mixed up two different events and stated the mix-up as fact. Meera had not been careless — she had trusted a voice that sounded like a textbook.

If she had opened her Class 7 history book and checked the same question, the right year was already there. The pause would have taken two minutes. It would have saved the marks, and it would have taught her the more useful habit: a fluent answer is still a draft.
